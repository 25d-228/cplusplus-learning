Chapter 18 — Synchronization and thread lifetime

Part 1 — Visibility and bounded ownership transfer

An owning batch can remain alive while two threads corrupt its contents. A queue can protect every pointer with a mutex and still lose accepted work on shutdown. A worker can finish its calculation and leave a joinable thread handle whose destruction terminates the program. These are three different failures: unsafe access, incorrect state transitions, and incomplete lifetime management.

We will make those differences observable before introducing threads into Strata. The final exercise transfers jobs valued 10, 20, and 12, drains to a sum of 42, and leaves a rejected late job valued 99 with its caller. It also stops blocked producers and consumers, transports worker exceptions, and cleans up when a later worker cannot be started. The queue is a teaching component, not the engine's query scheduler.

Learning objectives Trace the synchronization that makes a value visible; protect a multi-field invariant; distinguish notification from permission; transfer unique ownership through a bounded ring; separate draining from cancellation; and join every started thread before destroying anything it can access.

Prerequisites: the runnable programs and CMake setup from Chapters 1–5, especially borrowed references, RAII, moves, exceptions, and lambda captures. The engine remains exactly at Chapter 17. Nothing in this chapter makes its budgets, cache, or database safe for concurrent calls. The exercises include no engine headers and modify no engine source.

Implementation: 3.0 hours across both parts. Allocate approximately 0.75 hours to visibility and mutex programs, 1.25 to queue and thread ownership, and 1.0 to failure/wakeup checks and diagnosis. Reading is separate. These are working estimates, not minimum time requirements.

For each named file, concatenate its filename-labelled C++ blocks in reading order, with a blank line between blocks. Every function and header is supplied. The diagnostic is excluded from the normal build and test suite. Part 1 develops the visible-state and queue contracts; Part 2 completes the worker owner, tests, and build commands.

A thread is not a delayed function call

Constructing a std::thread starts a separate execution of its callable. The constructing thread continues independently; it does not wait for that callable to return. A lambda such as [&] { result = input + 22; } stores references to the surrounding variables it uses. Copying the callable does not extend those variables' lifetimes. We must keep them alive until the worker is finished.

The first program has one writer of result and reads it only after joining. Create the beginning of visibility.cpp:

The unnamed namespace keeps these helpers local to this translation unit. require throws on a failed check even in a Release build. The feature check asks for the C++20 standard-library atomic wait/notify facility used below; a compiler's acceptance of -std=c++20 alone is not that library test. The <version> feature-test interface lets us check the actual library rather than a compiler banner. An appropriate native Apple or LLVM toolchain is sufficient; no particular macOS release is required here.

The worker captures input and result by reference. Its expression cannot throw in this fixture. worker.join() waits for completion; after a successful join the handle is no longer joinable. We do not read result while the worker might be writing it. Thread construction and joining provide synchronization, rather than a guess about how quickly the worker runs. See thread construction and join.

Write A \prec B for an operation sequenced before another in one thread, and A \rightarrow_{\mathrm{sync}} B for a synchronization edge between threads. Combining those edges gives a happens-before relationship. Here the useful path is:

\text{write result} \prec \text{worker completion} \rightarrow_{\mathrm{sync}} \text{join returns} \prec \text{read result}.

The labels name operations, not elapsed times. Reading after a long sleep would not establish that path. A join performed after an unsynchronized conflicting read would not repair the earlier read either.

A plain increment is a read followed by computation and a write. Imagining two threads both reading 0 and both writing 1 explains a possible lost-update symptom, but is not a specification for an incorrect C++ program. Potentially concurrent conflicting accesses, at least one non-atomic, without the required happens-before ordering constitute a data race and produce undefined behavior. The language's data-race rule is stronger than “the answer may be slightly inaccurate.” volatile does not supply the missing cross-thread relationship.

Protect the relationship between fields

Our next object records a count and a sum. Every accepted contribution is two, so the representation invariant is

S = 2C,

where C is count and S is sum. Each of two threads contributes 5,000 times; the final values must be C=10{,}000 and S=20{,}000. These fixture bounds fit comfortably in uint64_t. We want readers to see the pair before or after a contribution, never halfway through it.

Before the next listing, introduce a small exception-transport mechanism. std::exception_ptr is a nullable owner referring to an exception object. Inside catch (...), std::current_exception() captures the current exception. After joining, std::rethrow_exception() raises it in the controlling thread. A worker's exception does not automatically propagate through join. An uncaught exception escaping the thread callable invokes termination. The exception propagation interface and the thread constructor contract describe these separate mechanisms.

Append the complete ledger and its check:

std::mutex gives exclusive access. std::lock_guard lock(ledger.mutex) acquires it immediately and releases it when the guard's scope ends, including exception unwinding. Class template argument deduction infers lock_guard<std::mutex>. The guard is named: a temporary guard destroyed at the end of its declaration would not protect the later statements.

All modifications and the in-loop invariant check use the same mutex. For example, thread A changes (7,14) to (8,16) while holding it. Thread B cannot enter its guarded region and inspect (8,14). A prior unlock synchronizes with a subsequent successful lock of that mutex; this supplies visibility as well as exclusion. See the mutex requirements.

The worker captures ledger by value, creating another shared_ptr handle, and worker_error by reference. Each handle keeps the same Ledger alive. The reference count does not lock the pointed-to fields. The mutex, not the shared ownership, makes the updates safe. Similarly, concurrent mutation of the same ordinary shared_ptr handle would require its own synchronization; separate handles sharing a control block are a different case. The shared-pointer specification distinguishes handle operations from access to the pointed-to object.

Only the worker writes worker_error; the caller reads it after joining. No extra mutex is needed for that access pattern. If the caller's own loop throws, its catch joins before rethrowing. The worker catches its own error and exits, so the caller never waits for an exception to magically cross the thread boundary. In the normal path both loops have ended before the final unguarded ledger read. If both loops failed, this simple fixture reports the caller's exception first.

Changing both fields to atomics would remove races on the individual fields, but two separate atomic stores still would not be one pair update. Even with strong ordering, a reader could observe one new field and one old field. For this representation a single mutex is the direct solution. Lock-free algorithms are not a prerequisite for correct concurrent software.

A one-shot flag can publish immutable data

An atomic is useful when its precise contract matches the problem. The next fixture has one writer, one reader, and one transition from not-ready to ready. After publication, the writer never changes the payload again.

Append the final portion of visibility.cpp:

std::atomic<bool> makes operations on ready atomic. The explicit release store publishes preceding writes; an acquire observation of that stored value orders subsequent reads. The release/acquire rule supplies the edge:

\text{write payload} \prec \text{store true, release} \rightarrow_{\mathrm{sync}} \text{observe true, acquire} \prec \text{copy payload}.

ready.wait(false, acquire) checks the atomic value and waits while it still compares equal to false. Its return follows observation of a different value; our only other value is the single published true. notify_one wakes a waiter to check; it is not publication by itself. If the store happens first, the wait observes the changed value without needing a remembered notification. See atomic wait and notification operations.

Notice that observed is copied before joining the writer. The acquire is therefore doing real work. Joining first would make this a poor demonstration because join would independently order the payload read. The check that can throw still occurs after join, keeping the reference captures alive safely.

Replacing acquire/release with relaxed operations would leave the non-atomic payload without this publication relationship. Resetting the flag and reusing the payload would introduce a different protocol: the writer could overwrite fields while a previous reader uses them. This example is deliberately not a reusable mailbox or an engine-generation publication algorithm.

Define ownership before defining the ring

We now transfer unique_ptr<Job> objects through a bounded queue. A successful push empties the caller's handle. A rejected push leaves that exact handle and job intact. Pop transfers ownership to one consumer. Empty jobs are forbidden, so an empty pop result can mean that this queue will deliver no further work.

The caller must exclusively own its handle during push; the queue's mutex cannot protect some other thread concurrently resetting that same caller handle. The job's bytes are initialized before submission and become consumer-owned after pop. No participant keeps a mutable alias and continues writing after transfer.

The queue has three states:

State New pushes Existing queued jobs Empty pop
open Wait for a free slot, then transfer Available to consumers Wait for state or work
draining Reject without moving Still available Return empty after drain
canceled Reject without moving Discard outside the mutex Return empty

Close means stop admitting and drain. Cancel means stop admitting and discard jobs not yet removed. Neither action joins a thread, and neither can revoke a job that a worker already owns. Calling close after cancel cannot reopen or restore the queue. Repeated close or cancel is permitted.

Create the beginning of job_queue.hpp:

The job's fail field is a deterministic processing failure used in Part 2. Job itself is not copied or moved; its unique_ptr is moved. inline static gives the exercise one shared counter definition across translation units. Atomic relaxed increments/decrements count live jobs without claiming to publish their contents. We check final zero after all owner scopes and joins have ended. This is not the engine's allocation accounting.

QueueProbe is a copy of observable queue state. producers and consumers count operations registered in a predicate wait; predicate_checks lets a test establish that a false condition was checked again after an extra notification. These are exercise diagnostics, not a scheduling interface.

Complete the header:

std::unique_lock<std::mutex> is an owning lock wrapper that also permits explicit unlocking and relocking. A condition-variable wait needs that ability: it must let another thread acquire the mutex and change the condition. A lock_guard deliberately has no such interface and suits shorter, non-waiting critical sections.

The mutex protects all ring slots, indices, count, state, and probe counters. The fixed capacity never changes. The condition variables do not separately own the state. space_ serves producers, item_ serves consumers, and observed_ serves the test's state rendezvous. No queue operation recursively takes the mutex; helpers receive an already-held lock.

For capacity C, queued count N, next read slot R, and next write slot W, the live ring obeys

0 \le N \le C,\qquad 0 \le R < C,\qquad 0 \le W < C.

The N slots reached from R by wrapped increments own jobs; the others are empty. Equal read/write indices alone cannot distinguish empty from full, so we keep the count. In the canceled state the representation is intentionally simpler: the slot vector is empty, N=0, and every operation checks cancellation before touching a slot.

With capacity two, follow the actual indices:

Operation Slots afterward Read Write Count
Start empty, empty 0 0 0
Push 10 10, empty 0 1 1
Push 20 10, 20 0 0 2
Pop empty, 20 1 0 1
Push 12 12, 20 1 1 2
Close 12, 20 1 1 2
Pop 12, empty 0 1 1
Pop empty, empty 1 1 0

The pop order is 10, 20, 12, even though the final physical slots held 12 before 20. Closing changes permission to submit, not the order of accepted jobs.

Allocate before concurrent transfer begins

Create the start of job_queue.cpp:

The capacity check runs before constructing the vector of empty handles. Allocation failure occurs before the queue can be shared. There is no vector growth during transfer. advance increments only a valid index below capacity; the conditional wraps the last index to zero without needing remainder or an unbounded monotonically growing position.

fetch_add(1, relaxed) and fetch_sub(1, relaxed) are indivisible updates of the counter; their returned previous values are unused. They do not authorize access to a job or prove it is safe to destroy a queue. After joining all participants, the fixture may read the final count; until then any reported count is only a momentary observation. Atomicity of the counter is not synchronization of all other memory.

A notification is a reason to inspect state

A full producer needs the condition “there is space or the queue no longer accepts work.” A consumer needs “there is an item or the state will not produce another one.” Waiting solely for space or an item would strand threads when shutdown changes only the queue state.

Append the producer wait helper:

The predicate form of wait repeats the condition check under the mutex. The underlying wait releases the mutex and blocks as one operation, then reacquires before continuing. This avoids a window in which a producer checks “full,” misses a notification, and only afterward begins to sleep. The same mutex must protect the condition's changes. A wake may be spurious, or another producer may take the slot first; notification therefore cannot be treated as a permit. See the condition-variable contract.

Registration happens while holding mutex_. An observer acquiring this mutex and seeing a producer registered knows the operation reached the predicate-wait protocol. It does not know whether an operating-system thread is physically asleep at that exact instant. That distinction is harmless: the queue cannot complete this full-state push until space or state changes. The catch restores diagnostic bookkeeping before propagating a catchable error; a failure to reacquire the mutex is a library termination case, not a recovery promise.

Append the analogous consumer helper:

Here the meaningful resource is a queued job, not a free slot. In both helpers observed_.notify_all() lets the controller inspect already-protected counters. Notifying while holding the mutex is allowed; an awakened observer acquires it before reading. The normal queue notifications below occur after unlocking, avoiding an immediate attempt to acquire a still-held lock. Neither placement replaces the predicate or the state transition.

Move only at the acceptance point

Append the complete push:

The if outside the helper is a fast-path test. It is not an unsafe one-time replacement for the wait loop: the helper itself performs a predicate wait. After waiting, we examine state again while holding the same lock. If close won the race, the function returns without touching job. If admission won, pointer move, index update, and count update all occur before unlocking. No observer can see the intermediate ring representation.

Consider close racing a push into a nonfull queue. Whichever takes the mutex and performs its transition first determines the result. A push accepted just before close remains accepted; close does not retrospectively reject it. The successful ring update is the operation's linearization point: we can reason about it as one event in an order consistent with these protected transitions.

Append the complete pop:

An open queue with zero count has already waited. The return-empty branch is therefore cancellation or a drained closed queue, not temporary lack of work. The local result becomes the sole owner before the slot is reused. Destruction of a consumer-owned job happens in the consumer, not inside this critical section.

The condition deliberately does not say “return empty whenever the queue is not open.” Such code would protect memory correctly but discard the promised drain semantics. Part 2 changes exactly that condition as a diagnostic mutation. A race detector cannot determine that the wrong shutdown policy was implemented.

Stop admission, then decide what remains owned

Append close:

We wake both classes of waiter. A full producer must discover rejection; an empty consumer must discover completion. Every waiting thread may need to exit, so notify_one is not enough for shutdown. A worker still holding a job can finish it and then observe the draining state on its next pop.

Append cancellation:

Swapping with a local, initially empty vector transfers ownership of all queued jobs without allocating new slots. The empty member vector is valid because canceled operations never index it. After unlocking, we wake waiters and destroy the abandoned jobs outside the mutex. A more complex job destructor could release other resources; keeping it out of the lock avoids turning cleanup into an unplanned nested critical section. This exercise's destructor only updates an atomic counter.

If a worker popped job 10 immediately before cancellation, that job is no longer in slots_. The abandoned vector cannot contain it. The worker may finish job 10 even after cancel returns. Cancellation is not rollback, forced termination, or proof that all users have stopped. The eventual owner must join them before destroying the queue. Concurrent cancellation calls are safe: after the first swap, the member vector is empty and later swaps have no queued owners to take.

Bound the backlog without pretending to bound a process

Let C be queue capacity, W the number of workers, and P the number of producers. Suppose each worker owns at most one removed job and each producer allocates at most one pending job before blocking in push. During ordinary processing, the participating jobs satisfy

J \le C + W + P.

The three terms are distinct owners: queued, running, and waiting to submit. For the Part 2 stress fixture, C=3, W=3, and P=3, giving at most nine such jobs despite 600 total transfers. A bound of three would forget both workers and blocked producers. Cancellation temporarily moves queued ownership to the canceling caller for destruction; it does not create another copy of a job.

This bound says nothing about jobs retained independently by callers, thread stacks, allocator metadata, vector capacity overhead, exceptions, or process resident memory. It is not Strata's charged query-memory admission policy. Blocking admission can also deadlock when the producer holds a resource needed by a consumer. The queue itself cannot diagnose that larger dependency cycle. We keep its critical sections short and hold no application mutex across push.

Observe waiting without using time as evidence

Complete job_queue.cpp:

await_waiters uses a protected predicate to establish a rendezvous. Its five-second timeout is only a watchdog reporting a broken test or an unusually stalled environment; waiting five seconds is never evidence that the desired state occurred. The predicate must actually be true. Part 2 also gives the whole test process a CTest timeout so a missing shutdown wakeup does not hang forever.

notify_without_change sends an extra notification without changing the queue. Tests wait for a later predicate evaluation and confirm that a full producer or empty consumer remains registered. This models tolerating a wakeup without permission. It does not claim to force a particular operating-system spurious wakeup or prove which notification caused a check.

The observation counters belong to this exercise. They add a condition variable and notifications that a production queue need not carry. They are used only at the fixture's modest operation counts, not as an unbounded telemetry API. Sharing the queue mutex prevents the tests from adding a race while observing one.

What must survive a shutdown request

The queue now knows how to change state and wake callers. It still cannot own their threads. Destroying it while a blocked push, pop, or notification uses its mutex and condition variables would violate its lifetime contract. A destructor that merely called close would not solve that problem: the waiters would be awake, not necessarily finished.

Part 2 — Join before destroying shared state provides the thread owner and verifies that references, exception slots, queue storage, and mutexes survive through the final join.
